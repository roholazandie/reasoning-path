1

Paths and best-of-k

A chain of thought is a path. Each sample leaves the prompt x, wanders through token space, and ends at an answer y(z). Some paths land in the set 𝒮x that the verifier accepts. Most do not.

px = Σz : y(z) ∈ 𝒮x π(z | x)

The success probability px adds up the probability of every path that lands. Here one sample lands – of the time.

With a verifier, you can sample k paths and keep any that lands. All k miss with probability (1 − px)k, so success rises with every extra sample.

Majority vote (self-consistency, Wang et al. 2022) needs no verifier: sample k paths and keep the answer that comes up most often. Every correct path gives the same answer, while wrong paths scatter over many different answers, so the vote works when the correct answer is the most common single answer. At 40 steps it climbs to about 59% at k = 31. At 36 steps one wrong answer is more common than the right one, and voting makes things worse: 7% falls to about 3%. Wang et al. reported +17.9 points on GSM8K. The vote line zigzags because even k gives more ties, which are broken by a coin flip.

Best-of-k scales the number of paths. Longer paths help only up to about 45 steps: past that, the drift carries the walks past 𝒮x, and success falls. The thinking budget is the toy version of s1's budget forcing (Muennighoff et al. 2025), which stops a model's thinking early or extends it by appending "Wait". Snell et al. (2024) found that the best way to spend test-time compute, for example revising one answer step by step or sampling many in parallel, depends on how hard the problem is, and that adapting the strategy to the estimated difficulty matched best-of-N with about 4× less compute.

More noise spreads the walks: a little helps them reach a target the drift misses, too much scatters them.

2

Training and generalization

Training changes the model so that one sample lands more often. Sample paths from every training prompt, keep the ones that land, and fine-tune on them. Then repeat.

∇θ px ≈ M / N · 1/M Σlanded ∇θ log πθ(zi | x)

With a 0/1 reward, one step of plain REINFORCE (no baseline) is one step of rejection-sampling fine-tuning on the paths that landed, scaled by the success rate M/N.

The search cost is paid during training and stored in the weights. This is amortization.

One set of weights serves every prompt, so the new prompt also improves. Its gain gets smaller as you drag it away from the training prompts.

Generalization width sets how far what is learned at one prompt reaches. Narrow (0.1): each prompt learns only from its own successes, so a prompt that never lands, like the top one, stays at 0%, and the new prompt does not move. Wide: prompts share what they learn, the top one borrows from its neighbours, and the new prompt improves almost as much as the training prompts.

Fewer samples per round make each round's fit noisier, so training needs more rounds and can stall when no sample lands.

The filter decides which answers count as good enough to train on. Too strict (0.5×) and one prompt never gets a kept answer, so it stays at 0%. Too loose (2.5×) and training copies near-misses: the prompts end at 20–38% instead of about 75%.

This loop has several names. STaR (Zelikman et al. 2022) used it to teach models to reason; ReST-EM (Singh et al. 2023) samples, keeps only answers that pass a binary check, fine-tunes, and repeats, and beat fine-tuning on human-written solutions for MATH and APPS. Expert iteration is the same idea in game playing.

In this toy, a walk is a Gaussian random walk on a fixed arc. Training learns an offset that bends the arc.

An imperfect verifier

The reward is only as good as the verifier. If the verifier accepts some wrong answers, training finds them, especially when they are easier to reach than the right ones.

The ellipse reaches back toward the prompt. Its wrong answers are the easiest to hit, so after training most walks land there.

Turn off the imperfect verifier. The same training now lands on the correct answers.

Reshape the verifier's region and watch the two rates under the figure. Training learns to hit whatever part of the region is easiest to reach. With the default shape, 68% of walks end on wrong answers. Lengthen the region to 0.6 and it covers more of the correct circle: wrong answers fall to 38%.

Reward hacking needs two things: a reward that is wrong somewhere, and a sampler that explores enough to find that place. More samples and longer RL make finding that place more likely.

Step 5 shows the same failure with a reward that people teach, not a program.

A warning from the other direction: Shao et al. (2025) found that random rewards raised Qwen2.5-Math-7B by 21.4 points on MATH-500, close to the 29.1 points of true rewards. It mostly did not work for Llama3 or OLMo2, and they trace it to GRPO's clipping amplifying habits the model already had. A gain after RL is not proof that the reward taught anything. This toy has no such pretrained habits, so it cannot show the effect.

4

Preferences and the reward model

Many tasks have no verifier: "be helpful" has no answer key. RLHF asks people to compare two answers, then trains a reward model to predict their choice.

Pick the answer you think is better. You do not know what "better" means here. Neither does the model. A hidden utility u(y) stands in for what people want.

The simulated labeler picks A with probability σ((u(A) − u(B)) / noise). The setting applies to new auto labels. With 50 labels, accuracy falls from about 91% at 0.25× to about 70% on average at 4×, with large swings from one set of labels to the next. Very precise labels also make the reward model steeper, so the same β pushes the policy further in step 5.

AI labeler (RLAIF, Lee et al. 2023): a model labels the pairs instead of people. It is cheap and consistent, and Lee et al. found it comparable to RLHF. But it has its own taste: here it also likes answers further up. Clear the labels and press the AI button four times (200 labels): the reward model still ranks about 92% of held-out pairs like the hidden utility, yet in step 5 RL usually lands far above the peak and the true utility gets worse. More AI labels do not fix it, because the taste is in every label.

This is the Bradley–Terry model. The reward model rφ is fit by logistic regression on the pairs: maximize Σ log σ(rφ(winner) − rφ(loser)).

Here rφ is the simplest reward model: a straight slope. It ranks about 90% of held-out pairs correctly. All pairs come from the base model, so it is only trustworthy there. Past the best answers, the slope keeps rising.

Overoptimization: train to the end for every β

The reward model is a proxy. Lower β lets the policy chase the proxy further from the data it learned from. The proxy keeps rising. The true utility rises, peaks, and then falls. This is Goodhart's law, measured by Gao et al. (2022).

6

DPO: skip the reward model

The KL-regularized objective has a closed-form best policy: π*(y) ∝ πref(y) exp(r(y)/β). DPO turns that around and fits the policy straight to the preference pairs.

DPO and RLHF both need preferences first.

or label them yourself in step 4

Each method has its own β, so you can compare them at the same KL instead of the same β. New set of labels replaces the labels with a fresh random set of the same size and retrains all three: DPO's result moves more from set to set.

ℒDPO = −𝔼 log σ( β log πθ(yw) / πref(yw) − β log πθ(yl) / πref(yl) )

This is the Bradley–Terry loss from step 4, with the reward written as β log πθ/πref. The policy is the reward model, so no sampling and no RL loop are needed.

Both methods learn only from the pairs. RLHF then samples answers from its own policy and trusts the reward model's extrapolation there; DPO never samples at all, so it only reshapes probability around the labelled answers.

IPO (Azar et al. 2023) points at a flaw in DPO: its loss keeps falling as the gap between winner and loser grows, with no finite minimum, so when preferences are certain it keeps pushing the loser's probability toward zero and the KL term stops mattering. IPO instead fits each pair's log-ratio gap to a fixed target, 1/(2τ). In this toy, DPO can keep pushing by narrowing its spread, and only a floor on the spread (0.02) stops it. With near-certain labels (labeler noise 0.25×), DPO's KL rises from 1.8 to 2.8, while IPO's stays near the base model (0.09 to 0.20). τ and β are not on the same scale, so use the sliders to compare at equal KL.

DPO also depends more on which pairs you happened to label. Over 20 sets of 200 labels at β = 0.3, its spread ranged from 75% to 155% of the base model's, and its true utility gain averaged 0.82, against 1.00 for RLHF. Collect a new set of labels in step 4 and retrain to see it move.

The 0/1 reward shrinks the spread fast: the policy stops exploring as soon as it finds the verifier. This is entropy collapse. Cui et al. (2025) measured it in reasoning models: entropy drops early in RL, performance saturates with it, and they fit performance as R = −a·eH + b, so performance is bought with entropy until none is left. The reward model's straight slope gives no reason to narrow, so the RLHF side stays at 100%.

Reward hacking and defenses

Reward hacking means the policy scores high on the reward but low on what you wanted. You saw two causes: holes in a verifier (step 3) and a reward model that extrapolates (step 5). Here is a third cause, and two defenses.

Labelers who prefer the wrong thing

People who compare answers often prefer the longer one, even when it is not better. Here the x-axis stands for length. On the right, the labelers add a liking for length to their judgment. The hidden utility is the same on both sides.

Singhal et al. (2023) found this in real RLHF: much of the reward gain came from longer answers, and a reward that only counts length reproduced most of RLHF's improvement. The reward model learns exactly what the labelers did, length liking included, and RL follows it. The policy gives longer answers, and they are worse than with fair labelers. A stronger KL penalty limits the damage by stopping earlier, but it cannot remove the bias: the reward model still points toward length, because the bias is in the data, not in the optimizer.

Two defenses

At β = 0.1, plain RLHF runs past the best answers (step 5). Two common defenses:

  • Ensemble (a variant of Coste et al. 2023, who found that conservative ensembles practically eliminate overoptimization for best-of-n). Fit 8 reward models, each on a resample of the labels. Optimize their mean minus 3 × their spread. The models agree where there is data and disagree away from it, so the policy stays near the data.
  • Iterated RLHF. Every 30 steps, label 150 fresh pairs from the current policy and refit the reward model on them. The reward model is always accurate where the policy is now.

WARM (Ramé et al. 2024) averages the weights of several reward models into one, instead of averaging their outputs; a policy trained against it won 79.4% of comparisons against one trained on a single reward model. For this toy's straight-line reward models, averaging weights and averaging outputs give the same thing, the ensemble's mean, so WARM adds nothing here. Its advantage needs real networks.

Try the slider. At 0, with these labels, both defenses stop the runaway: the ensemble gains 0.65 and iterated RLHF 0.93, against −0.59 for plain RLHF. The ensemble is not reliable, though: with other label sets it fails about two times in five. At 2, the ensemble fails (−0.48): every member learned the same liking for length, so they agree, and agreement is not caution. Iterated RLHF still does best (0.56), because near the best answer the quality differences between fresh pairs outweigh the length preference, but it already drifts toward longer answers. At 5 and above, the length preference wins there too, and iterated RLHF drifts to long answers (−0.74).

In this toy, each reward model is a straight slope, so the iterated version refits on the newest round only. Real reward models are large networks trained on all rounds.

GEPA: reflective prompt evolution

Every method so far changed the weights. GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2025) leaves the weights alone and rewrites the prompt. It runs a few tasks, reads in words what went wrong, and edits the instructions.

The system has two modules, like two LLM calls in a pipeline. Each line steers the answers. The numbers are what the line does in this toy; GEPA itself only sees the words.

Why text feedback is cheap. A 0/1 reward says only that a rollout missed. "Ended 0.21 too low" says which way to move and roughly how far. One rollout with text feedback carries more than many rollouts of pass or fail.

The reflector here is a simple rule standing in for an LLM. It reads the misses, notices whether they grow with task height, and writes one line. Its sense of "how much" is rough on purpose.

What the controls do (median over 6 runs, rollouts to 85% of the best). Reflector quality 0, where every edit is random, needs about 4,900; 0.35 needs 1,800; the default 0.7 needs 1,100; 1 needs 860. With text feedback off, the reflector sees only pass or fail and needs about 3,500. A minibatch of 1 stalls: a child must beat its parent on one rollout, which says almost nothing. A minibatch of 6 needs about 620. The samples per task on the Pareto set make scoring cheaper or more exact; in this toy the two roughly balance.

    What RL can and cannot add

    RL with a verifier makes the model land more often on its first try. Does it also make the model able to solve problems it could not solve before? And what if the verifier almost never says yes?

    RL raises pass@1 and can lower pass@k

    Ten tasks, one shared policy. Each task needs a different correction, and the policy can learn only one offset and how widely it spreads. pass@k is the chance that at least one of k attempts at a task lands, averaged over tasks.

    pass@k, mean over 10 tasks

    RL finds the offset that serves the most tasks and then narrows the spread to hit them every time. The tasks it gave up on drop to zero. The base model is wide and clumsy, but it reaches every task sometimes, so with enough attempts it wins.

    Yue et al. (2025) found the same pattern in real models: RLVR-trained models win at small k, the base models win at large k, and the reasoning the trained model shows was already within the base model's reach. A penalty for narrowing keeps more tasks alive at the cost of pass@1.

    Outcome reward vs process reward

    Make the verifier small (radius 0.08, as in step 7) and the base model almost never lands. An outcome reward, 0/1 at the end, then learns only from the rare lucky paths. A process reward scores the steps on the way: here, how many of 5 checkpoints the path passes inside a corridor around a correct path. Both use the same RL loop with a weak KL penalty (β = 0.01).

    Step 0. Landing rate: outcome –, process –.
    Landing rate during training

    The process reward gives partial credit long before any path lands, so it learns faster at first: it reaches 20% landing in about 50 steps, against about 100 for the outcome reward. Then it stalls near 60%, while the outcome reward keeps climbing to about 95%. The process reward pays for staying in the corridor, which is only a stand-in for landing, and optimizing a stand-in stops short of the real goal.

    Lightman et al. (2023) trained process and outcome reward models from human labels (PRM800K, 800,000 step-level labels) and used them to pick the best of many sampled solutions; process supervision "significantly outperforms" outcome supervision there, solving 78% of a MATH test subset. This toy instead uses a hand-built corridor as an RL reward, so it shows the trade-off, not their result. A corridor around a wrong path would teach the wrong path: the step 3 problem one level down.

    RL algorithms and best-of-N

    The same 0/1 task as step 1, but now the policy also chooses how long to think: its chain length is sampled around a mean that training can move. The algorithms differ only in how they turn rewards into weights on each answer.

    • REINFORCE: weight = reward − group mean.
    • RLOO: weight = reward − mean of the other answers in the group.
    • GRPO: weight = (reward − group mean) / group spread, then divided by the answer's own length.
    • Dr. GRPO: GRPO without both divisions.
    • PPO-clip: four updates per batch; an answer stops contributing once its probability ratio passes 1.2 while its advantage is positive, or drops below 0.8 while it is negative. Here it uses the group mean as its baseline, with no value network.

    Mean chain length during training (steps)

    Chains grow longer. The mean chain length rises during training, from 40 steps to about 44 with REINFORCE and 47 with PPO-clip. DeepSeek-R1-Zero (DeepSeek-AI 2025) thought for longer and longer as RL training went on, with no instruction to do so. Here the reason is plain: chains of about 45 steps land most often, so RL moves toward them. Longer is not better in itself, as step 1 showed.

    The length bias. GRPO divides each answer's loss by its length. A long wrong answer then costs less per step than a short one, so training stops pushing away from long wrong answers. Here GRPO's wrong answers end longer than its right ones, while Dr. GRPO's end shorter. Liu et al. (2025) found this in R1-Zero-style training: GRPO "artificially increases response length (especially for incorrect outputs)", and removing the two divisions fixes it.

    The same update three times. REINFORCE with a group mean, RLOO and Dr. GRPO weight answers the same way up to a constant factor, and the Adam optimizer ignores constant factors. REINFORCE and RLOO give identical runs here. Dr. GRPO's run starts identical too, then drifts a little, because floating-point rounding changes which random answers land later in training. Ahmadian et al. (2024) found REINFORCE-style RLOO beat PPO for RLHF while being simpler.

    Clipping. Raise the learning rate to 0.1 (release the slider to rerun): REINFORCE falls to about 3% success and GRPO to about 7%, while PPO-clip keeps about 14%. At 0.3 all of them break.

    GRPO comes from DeepSeekMath (Shao et al., 2024). In this toy, chain length varies by ±20 steps; in real models responses range from hundreds to thousands of tokens, which makes the length division matter far more.

    Best-of-N against RL, at the same KL

    Best-of-N needs no training: sample N answers from the base model and keep the one the reward model scores highest. Its distance from the base model is log N − (N − 1)/N. This uses step 5's reward model, fitted on a fixed set of 200 labels.

    At the same KL, best-of-N reaches more true utility than RL here, and it overoptimizes too: past N = 64 the true utility falls while the proxy keeps rising. Gao et al. (2022) reported the same two facts for real reward models. One reason in this toy: RL can only shift and narrow a Gaussian, while best-of-N can cut off its low-reward side.

    In this toy the answers are continuous, so log N − (N − 1)/N is the exact KL. Beirami et al. (2024) showed that for real models, whose outputs are discrete, it is only an upper bound on the true KL.
